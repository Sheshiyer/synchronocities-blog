## The Wood Wide Web

Mycorrhizal networks — the symbiotic associations between fungal hyphae and plant roots — extend this computational architecture across entire ecosystems.

Approximately 90% of land plants form mycorrhizal associations. The fungus provides water and mineral nutrients to the plant root. The plant provides carbohydrates to the fungus. But the relationship is not binary. The fungal hyphae connect multiple plants — often of different species — into a shared network.

Through this network:
- Carbon moves from one tree to another. A Douglas fir seedling shaded by older trees receives carbon from the roots of those trees, transmitted through the fungal network.
- Chemical warnings propagate. When a tree is attacked by insects, it releases chemical signals that travel through the mycelial network to neighboring trees, which then upregulate their defensive compounds.
- Nitrogen and phosphorus are allocated based on need, not proximity. The network redistributes resources across the community.

The architecture: a distributed resource management system running on a biological substrate, with the fungal network serving as both the communication channel and the resource allocator.

The network is not neutral. The fungus benefits from maintaining connectivity. A fungus connected to multiple plants has access to multiple carbohydrate sources and can redistribute resources from well-supplied plants to plants that are struggling — keeping its host community healthy and its own nutrient supply diversified. This is portfolio management implemented in hyphae.

## Decomposition as Computation

Decomposition is fundamental.
The **cellulose** and **lignin** molecules that comprise plant cell walls are notoriously difficult to break down, having evolved to resist decomposition. This resistance is a result of their complex structure, which includes beta-1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose and a three-dimensional network of cross-linked lignin molecules. Without organisms capable of decomposing these molecules, dead plant matter would accumulate indefinitely, causing a halt in the **carbon cycle**. Fungi, particularly **basidiomycetes** and **ascomycetes**, have evolved to fill this niche, secreting enzymes such as **cellulases**, **lignin peroxidases**, and **laccases** that break down these polymers into simpler molecules. The **oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)**, for example, is a well-studied species that produces a range of enzymes capable of degrading lignin and cellulose.
