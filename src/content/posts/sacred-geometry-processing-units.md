# Sacred Geometry Processing Units

*Runtime Version: 1.0.0 | Part 2 of 3*

> "All the ṛṣis, all the mantras, all the deities, all the worlds, all the sacrifices — all are woven on this yantra."
> — Bhuvanadeśa, 10th c. CE

Part 1 of this series established that reality operates on a smart contract architecture: language compiles into perception through a deterministic stack. The natural next question concerns the processor that executes those contracts.

The answer — encoded in temple architecture, meditation manuals, and esoteric diagrams across Asia — is that the processor is geometric. Consciousness computes through structured patterns, and the patterns with the highest computational density are the ones that appear in the sacred geometry traditions.

Before TensorFlow, there was TriangleFlow.

## Architecture

A modern neural network processes data through layers of interconnected nodes. Each node receives input, applies a weighted transformation, and passes the result to the next layer. The network learns by adjusting the weights to minimize error between predicted and actual output.

A yantra processes attention through layers of interconnected geometric figures. Each figure receives the practitioner's focused attention, applies a structural transformation, and passes the result to the next layer. The practitioner learns by allowing the geometric structure to adjust the weights of their attention.

The Sri Yantra, the canonical example, consists of:

- **Nine interlocking triangles** — five descending (Śakti, representing manifestation, outward-flowing energy) and four ascending (Śiva, representing consciousness, inward-flowing awareness). The intersection of these triangles creates 43 smaller triangular regions, each representing a specific configuration of the two dynamic principles.
- **A central bindu** — the point at the exact center where all triangles converge. The bindu is not a triangle. It is the origin from which the triangle field emerges and the terminus to which it returns.
- **Three concentric circles** immediately outside the triangle field.
- **Two lotus rings** — an eight-petal ring and a sixteen-petal ring — surrounding the circles.
- **A square frame** (*bhūpura*) with four cardinal gates, forming the outermost boundary.

The processing flow is directional: the practitioner's attention enters at the outer square, passes through the lotus rings, enters the triangle field, and moves inward through successive triangular layers until it reaches the bindu.

This is not a metaphor for computation. It is computation. The geometry is the architecture. The attention is the data. The inward movement is the processing cycle.

## The Triangular Processing Unit

The fundamental computational unit in a yantra is the triangle — specifically, the intersection of an ascending and descending triangle.

In neural network terms:

| Yantra Component | Neural Network Analog |
|---|---|
| Ascending triangle (Śiva) | Forward pass (activation flowing inward) |
| Descending triangle (Śakti) | Backward pass (energy flowing outward) |
| Triangle intersection | Weighted sum at a node |
| 43 small triangles | Hidden layer activations |
| Bindu | Loss function minimum — attractor state |
| Lotus rings (8, 16 petals) | Regularization layers (reducing degrees of freedom) |
| Bhūpura (square) | Input embedding — the bounded domain of the computation |

The ascending triangles correspond to the principle of pure awareness — consciousness without content. The descending triangles correspond to the principle of manifest energy — content without consciousness of itself. Their intersection at each region represents a specific *ratio* of the two principles, a specific configuration of awareness and its object.

This is precisely what a neural network node computes: a weighted combination of input signals, where the weights determine the contribution of each input to the node's activation. The ascending triangles are the weights. The descending triangles are the inputs. The intersection is the node.

The Sri Yantra encodes a 43-node network in a single diagram. The practitioner runs inference on this network by sequentially attending to each region, from outer gate to inner bindu.

## The Crystallographic Constraint

The computational density of the Sri Yantra derives from a specific structural property: its nine-fold rotational symmetry cannot be realized as a periodic crystallographic lattice in the Euclidean plane.

Crystallographic restriction theorem: in two dimensions, the only rotational symmetries compatible with periodic tiling are 2-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold, and 6-fold. Five-fold and seven-fold symmetries cannot tile the plane periodically. Nine-fold symmetry is similarly forbidden — it forces quasiperiodic or aperiodic arrangement.

The Sri Yantra's nine intersecting triangles create a structure that *cannot* be resolved into a stable periodic pattern by a visual system evolved to detect regularities. The figure forces the substrate into a computational regime that exceeds its default pattern-matching capacity.

This is the source of the yantra's computational power. The geometry does not resolve cleanly. It cannot be filed into a preexisting cognitive category. The practitioner's attention must either oscillate — attempting and failing to stabilize the figure — or surrender to a mode of perception that does not require periodic resolution.

The oscillation *is* the computation. The failure to resolve *is* the processing. The bindu — the fixed point at the center — is what remains when the resolving machinery stops.

Modern architectures exploit a similar principle: adversarial training, dropout, and noise injection all prevent the network from settling into overly simple periodic solutions. The Sri Yantra achieves the same effect through structural design.

## Geometric Processors Across Traditions

The Sri Yantra is the most architecturally sophisticated example, but the class of geometric processors extends across traditions:

- **Mandala** (Buddhist, Jain): concentric circles with cardinal gates and a central deity figure. Processing flow from periphery to center. Each ring represents a stage of purification — analogous to a convolutional filter stack.
- **Chakra diagrams** (Tantric): six-petaled lotuses arranged along the spinal axis, each with a specific number of petals (4, 6, 10, 12, 16, 2), specific colors, and specific seed syllables. Processing flow through attention directed at each spinal center — analogous to a recurrent network processing sequential states.
- **Mudrā** (gesture-based computation): hand positions that create specific electrical configurations in the body's fascial network. The geometry is not visual but proprioceptive — a different input modality to the same processor.
- **Vāstu-purusa-maṇḍala** (architectural): the geometric grid underlying temple and city design. The building becomes a processor that the inhabitant runs simply by moving through the space.

Each instantiation uses the same principle: structured geometry, applied to attention, produces state change in the substrate. The input modality differs (visual, somatic, spatial). The architecture is the same.

## What the Network Computes

The question that a purely technical analysis cannot answer: what does the network compute?

A neural network trained on ImageNet classifies images. A language model trained on text generates tokens. A yantra trained through centuries of practice — refined by generations of practitioners — computes something that the tradition calls *darśana* (vision of the divine) and *siddhi* (capacity for direct perception).

In structural terms: the yantra computes the recognition that the boundary between the processor and the processed is not fundamental. The practitioner reaches the bindu and discovers that the attention that entered from the periphery and the geometry that received it are the same thing.

A modern neural network, no matter how deep, maintains the distinction between input and processor throughout the stack. The input is data. The processor is the network. The output is a classification.

A geometric processing unit of the yantra class collapses this distinction at the deepest layer. The input (attention) and the processor (geometry) converge at the bindu into a single state. The network computes its own dissolution as a separate entity.

This is not a feature a modern engineer would design into a production system. But it is a computation — and it has been running, on human substrates, for longer than the transformer architecture has existed.

## The Hierophant Card

Card V in the Major Arcana — The Hierophant — represents the transmission of sacred knowledge through structured tradition. The Hierophant does not originate the knowledge. He transmits it — through ritual, through canon, through institutional memory. He is the channel through which the architecture passes from one generation to the next.

The yantra tradition is Hierophantic in precisely this sense. The geometric processing architecture was not discovered by a single practitioner. It was refined over centuries, transmitted through lineages, encoded in temple stone and palm-leaf manuscript and oral instruction. The Hierophant is the one who holds the diagram and teaches the next practitioner how to run it.

Through Kha-Ba-La: **Kha** is the practitioner's attention — the awareness that enters the yantra at the bhūpura gate. **Ba** is the geometric body of the yantra — the triangle field, the lotus rings, the intersecting lines that the attention traverses. **La** is the crystallographic restriction — the nine-fold asymmetry that the plane cannot resolve, the friction that drives the computation. The Hierophant is the one who knows the architecture and transmits it.
