# A Record Before an Explanation

Imagine receiving four words: “Can we talk tomorrow?”

You reply, “Of course.” The phone goes down. An imaginary committee convenes to decide what you have done wrong, with no agenda and remarkably strong attendance.

One account of the evening might read: “That message made me anxious because I’m always waiting for something to go wrong.” Another might read: “I saw those four words, noticed pressure in my chest and felt less settled than before. I replied immediately. I thought the sender was unhappy with me.”

The second account keeps several things distinguishable. There are the words, a reported sensation, a reply and a reading of the sender’s intention. The reading may prove accurate. At this point in the invented scene, we do not know.

A five-field record gives those details separate places before an explanation gathers them into a persuasive whole.

## A notebook with a little room left

A journal can hold association, memory, argument and emotion. A short log selects a few details so that they remain available for comparison later. Both have uses. The form proposed here names five fields: **trigger, signal, felt shift, action and story**.

The columns are a writing arrangement. They cannot measure neural events or establish the sequence in which experience unfolded. A story may have been present from the first glance at the screen. Putting it last gives it a place on the page, not a birth certificate.

The discipline is to write what is available without becoming more certain than the recollection permits. A blank field can be a faithful entry. A beautifully completed table can be five small inventions wearing the same border.

## Trigger: the event you can describe

In our scene, a message arrived saying, “Can we talk tomorrow?”

“The sender was trying to unsettle me” adds an intention that the wording does not establish. Put that reading in the story field, along with any evidence for it. The words themselves need their own place.

Recording an event still involves selection. Approximate time, circumstances and actual wording can make the selection easier to inspect. If the wording is uncertain, say so: “I remember a request to talk tomorrow.” Quotation marks cannot restore a sentence that has gone missing.

## Signal: what you noticed

A sensation, if noticed, can be recorded in ordinary language: pressure in the chest, warmth in the face, a clenched jaw or a change in breathing. These describe reported experience; their cause remains another question.

“Nothing clear” and “I did not notice” belong here too. The form does not require a bodily performance. If “anxious” is the honest description available, use it. An emotion word is more useful than a fictional anatomical detail supplied to impress the worksheet.

This matters especially on rereading. A page full of precise sensations may look authoritative. Its value depends on what was actually noticed and remembered, including the less impressive gaps.

## Felt shift: what seemed different

Perhaps you felt settled before the message and preoccupied afterward. Perhaps the evening was already tense and no separate change was clear. This field holds that remembered comparison.

The order can remain uncertain. You may have noticed a feeling after reading without knowing when it began. You may be reconstructing the contrast while writing. Keep that uncertainty attached, rather than upgrading a recollection into an instrument trace.

## Action: what happened next

“Replied ‘Of course’ immediately.” “Asked what the sender wanted to discuss.” “Opened the conversation again.” “Left the message until morning.” Each describes an action that could be examined afterward.

An impulse can also be recorded without acquiring the status of a sent message: “Wanted to ask whether something was wrong; did not ask.” The difference may matter. It does not need an immediate moral verdict.

Later consequences go in a note beneath the fields. A missed task that evening is another event. Giving it its own line keeps an entire evening from disappearing inside “I handled that badly.”

## Story: the meaning you assigned

“The sender was unhappy with me.” “I expected a difficult conversation.” “I thought a delayed reply would look rude.”

A story may be accurate. Keeping it in a separate field lets us ask what supported it at the time, and what became available afterward. We can preserve an interpretation without either crowning or humiliating it.

Here is the complete illustrative record:

| Field | Illustrative entry |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Received “Can we talk tomorrow?” |
| Signal | Noticed pressure in the chest |
| Felt shift | Settled to preoccupied, as remembered afterward |
| Action | Replied “Of course” immediately |
| Story | Thought the sender was unhappy with me |

The sender has yet to tell us what they meant. Tomorrow’s conversation might concern an ordinary scheduling question. That information belongs beside the record. It need not become an opportunity to ridicule yesterday’s concern. Yesterday had four words to work with; tomorrow has more.

## Let differences survive the comparison

If you want to try the form, a few brief records of low-stakes events are enough to begin asking questions. Leave fields blank where needed. Stop if the exercise becomes distressing, or choose another form of reflection if this one is a poor fit. There is no quota of revelations.

On comparison, look for what recurs and what differs. Two similar entries invite closer attention without proving a stable personal architecture. If one message prompted an immediate reply and another did not, what else was different? Sender, context, fatigue, available time? The notebook can keep those questions available; it cannot settle them by arranging the rows neatly.

You might notice a recurrence. You might find that the selection leaves out the most important part of an encounter. Then the method itself has something to learn. Five columns cannot contain a relationship, establish a motive or replace a conversation.

What they can do is leave a route back from the explanation to the words on the screen, the reply that was sent and the uncertainty that remained. When tomorrow’s information arrives, there is still somewhere to put it.
