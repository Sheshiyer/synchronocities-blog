# Why Insight Isn’t Change

In an imagined exchange, someone asks whether you can take on one more piece of work. The week is full. You know the week is full. You have already given an impressively articulate account of why you agree to things you resent.

“Yes,” you say.

The calendar acquires a new obligation. Your explanation remains excellent.

Later, it offers familiar possibilities: usefulness has become a way of belonging; a brief refusal feels harder to bear than a long commitment; disappointing someone now seems worse than disappointing several people on Friday. Any of these readings might help. None tells us, by itself, what will happen when the next request arrives.

The distance between an account and an occurrence is where this essay begins.

## The explanation has a comfortable chair

Giving a difficulty a name can be a relief. Something previously shapeless becomes discussable. A person can ask for help, see a conflict more clearly or stop treating every repetition as an inexplicable personal defect. That is real work.

It can also become a very comfortable place to remain. The account grows more nuanced while the week grows less possible. We learn to narrate the trap with such fluency that the narration starts to feel like an exit.

Keep two questions available: what do I understand now, and what happened when the relevant situation returned? Each asks for a different kind of evidence. A clearer description can answer the first. The second needs an occurrence, with its inconvenient particulars still attached.

This is why a modest account may sometimes help more than a grand one. “I have no boundaries” describes an entire person and leaves them rather little to do. “When a request arrives without notice, I agree before checking the week” points to an exchange we can examine. Who asked? What had already been promised? Was there time to answer later? What would refusing cost?

The smaller question has handles.

## What executes

Software offers a useful comparison. A description of a program can be elegant; running it introduces the conditions under which it must actually do something. The word *runtime* carries that distinction into this inquiry as a metaphor.

A person has relationships, obligations and a history that no script can adequately contain. Still, “what executes?” is a useful interruption when a theory of oneself begins to stand in for a particular response. The imagined person has explained their agreement. Now we can ask what took place between the request and the yes.

Perhaps the answer was given before the calendar was consulted. Perhaps the requester controls their employment. Perhaps the work is urgent and no one else is available. Each possibility changes the inquiry. An explanation built entirely around people-pleasing may overlook a very material arrangement of power. The calendar cannot negotiate an unsafe workplace on its own, however tasteful its colour coding.

Specificity may reveal room for a different answer. It may reveal a need for support or a change in workload. We need to know which problem we are asking an individual to solve.

## Seeing, acting, holding

Tryambakam Noesis uses **Kha**, **Ba** and **La** as a house lens for these questions: witnessing or authoring capacity; the embodied carrier of action; and inertia, resistance and the conditions through which a form continues. They organize an interpretation here rather than establish a universal law of change.

In the work-request scene, Kha asks whether the agreement can be noticed while there is still a choice. Ba asks what the person can actually say or do. La asks what makes that action difficult, and what would allow it to happen again.

Consider a small sentence: “Let me check the week before I answer.”

It does not decide the request. It makes checking possible. This is a considerably smaller undertaking than becoming an entirely new person by Thursday.

Now the arrangement around the sentence matters. Will the requester accept a later answer? Is there somewhere reliable to see existing commitments? Would a shared workload conversation do more than another private resolution? A prepared phrase may be useful, but it needs conditions in which it can be spoken and acted upon.

La gives resistance a place in the account. Some resistance may preserve an old habit. Some may point to an actual consequence: lost income, conflict, a task left with someone less able to carry it. Before treating resistance as an obstacle to remove, find out what it is holding.

## Where the cost goes

A quick yes can keep the present exchange smooth. Its cost may arrive later as a missed deadline, an evening of resentment or work quietly transferred to someone else. Refusal has costs too. Responsibility needs an account of both.

What did this answer preserve? Who carried what it cost? What information was missing? These questions are less flattering than an elegant origin story and more useful for deciding what happens next.

An explanation of the yes cannot erase its consequences. Equally, a difficult consequence cannot establish how free the person was to choose. The scene must hold both the decision and its conditions without turning either into a complete excuse for ignoring the other.

## Give the proposal somewhere to meet the world

If a low-stakes version of this exchange is familiar, an optional experiment can stay small. Describe the response as an action, choose one possible alternative and see what happens on a few ordinary occasions. Checking the calendar before answering is specific enough to recognize afterward. “Become better at boundaries” leaves rather more room for creative accounting.

Record occasions when the alternative was forgotten, unavailable or unhelpful as well as those when it occurred. Uncertain sequence can remain uncertain. There is no requirement to revisit painful material, and the exercise can be stopped; it is a writing inquiry rather than a treatment.

The question on returning to the record is whether the proposal fitted the situation. Did checking produce useful information? Did it merely postpone an answer already known? Was the actual issue a workload that needed a shared decision? A failed proposal may reveal a poor fit. No clear pattern is also an answer worth keeping.

“I understand myself better” and “I still agreed before checking” can both be true. One contribution has happened; another question remains. Self-authorship, as used here, includes participation in that unfinished work, with whatever choice and support the circumstances allow.

The next request will have a person, a time and a cost attached. Let those particulars have a say in what the explanation becomes.
