# The Messenger Has More Than One Address

Mercury is an awkward guest for a tidy lexicon. It arrives as a metal, a messenger, a planet and an alchemical principle. Give it one labelled drawer and three of its relatives are left standing in the corridor.

Steven A. Young sets out those four senses in *A Fool's Wisdom*. The distinction is worth keeping before asking what connects them. A dictionary can tell us which Mercury a sentence intends. An essay has the more troublesome job of deciding why a passage moves from one sense to another, and whether anything survives the crossing.

The messenger interests me here. A message needs somewhere to go, and passage changes it. A thought carried into speech meets a mouth, another person's attention, the furniture of a particular language. What seemed complete in silence can arrive missing a leg. A devastating reply rehearsed in the shower enjoys the generous cooperation of an imaginary audience.

Consider a deliberately constructed example. Someone says, “I need space.” One listener hears a request for an evening alone. Another hears the announcement of an ending. The sentence has travelled a very short distance and acquired two different futures. The example leaves us with a practical question: what would let the listener discover which message actually arrived?

Young's alchemical Mercury gives that question a vocabulary of mediation. In his discussion of the Three Primes, its associations shift with the application: receptivity and mind in one passage, spirit and liquor in herbal work. Reading those passages together asks for attention to context. A metal, a principle and a distilled spirit can be related within his account while remaining different things.

I would use that difference to keep an inquiry moving. When an impression becomes a conviction, what carried it? A remembered sentence, a bodily alarm, a teacher's authority, the pleasure of finding a pattern? Those carriers change what the impression can mean. The question gets less useful if “Mercury” becomes a decorative name for every process we haven't understood yet.

The same care matters when bringing one framework beside another. Kha–Ba–La offers my working questions of witnessing, embodied carriage and resistance. Young's Three Primes organise another set of relations. Their shared count gives us a place to compare, but the interesting work begins where an easy alignment fails. Does receptivity belong with the witness, with the carrier, or with the encounter between them? A premature answer would make the comparison wonderfully portable and rather empty.

There is room here for negation with a purpose. If a role fails to describe a person, we can loosen the identification and look again. We needn't rush to supply a more impressive identity. “I am the witness” can become another badge if it closes the very question that disturbed the first one. The inquiry may leave a little space without immediately renting it to a theory.

That is the relation I want to try: mediation as a place where meaning becomes answerable to what receives it. It is an interpretive proposal, and a conversation could expose its limits. In the constructed example, the listener could offer their understanding back: an evening alone, or an ending? The reply would give both people something more particular to work with than the authority of the listener's first reading.

Mercury can keep its several addresses. The useful question is which door a particular sentence has reached.

Source: Steven A. Young, *A Fool's Wisdom*, Chapter 15, “Mercury the Messenger,” and Chapter 20, “The Tria Prima.”
