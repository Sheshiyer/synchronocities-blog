# A Record Before an Explanation

*Five fields for keeping an event, a response and a story distinguishable.*

Imagine receiving a short message: “Can we talk tomorrow?” You reply, “Of course,” then spend part of the evening composing explanations for what the conversation might be about.

One version of the record says: “That message made me anxious because I’m always waiting for something to go wrong.” Another says: “I read those four words. I noticed pressure in my chest. I felt less settled than before. I replied immediately. I thought the sender was unhappy with me.”

The second account is longer, but it makes fewer things identical. The message is one thing. The sensation is another. The reply is an action. The prediction about the sender is an interpretation. None has to disappear for the others to become clearer.

This is an invented example. Its purpose is to introduce a recording method: keep a few aspects of an ordinary event in separate fields before deciding what the event means.

## A notebook can hold more than one kind of record

A journal can make room for memory, association, argument and emotion. A short log asks for a narrower kind of attention. It deliberately leaves some of the story unfinished so that particular details remain available for comparison.

There is no need to make the log superior to the journal. They ask different questions. A page of reflection might be exactly what a situation requires. A five-field record is useful to this inquiry because it gives each distinction a named place.

The method proposed here has five fields: **trigger, signal, felt shift, action and story**. The arrangement is a writing convention, not a measurement of neural events. It cannot tell you exactly what your nervous system did, or establish that experience unfolded in the order in which the columns appear.

Its discipline is modest: say what belongs in each field, admit what you do not remember, and avoid allowing an explanation to become the event it explains.

## 1. Trigger: what happened

Describe the event in a short sentence. In the invented example, the trigger is receiving a message that said, “Can we talk tomorrow?”

"The sender was trying to unsettle me" adds an intention the message itself does not establish. That interpretation may matter; give it a separate place in the story field, alongside any evidence that supports it.

An event description is not a view from nowhere. You are still selecting details and recalling what occurred. The aim is to make the selection inspectable. Actual words, approximate time and relevant circumstances give a later reader—including you—something more particular than a conclusion about someone’s character.

If the words are uncertain, say so. “I remember it as a request to talk tomorrow” is a better record than quotation marks around words you cannot verify.

## 2. Signal: what you noticed

Record a sensation if one was noticed: pressure in the chest, warmth in the face, a change in breathing, a clenched jaw. These are descriptions of reported experience, not explanations of its cause.

You do not have to discover a sensation. “Nothing clear” or “I did not notice” are usable entries. A column is an invitation to look, not a demand that the expected phenomenon appear.

Emotion words are not forbidden. You can write “anxious” if that is the description available. The useful distinction is between the word and any additional detail you can honestly report. Do not replace an honest broad description with invented bodily precision because the form seems to reward it.

## 3. Felt shift: what seemed different

Describe a contrast, if you remember one. Perhaps you felt settled before reading and preoccupied afterward. Perhaps the message arrived during an already tense evening and no separate shift was clear.

“Less settled” describes a remembered contrast. A physiological explanation would need a different kind of evidence.

This field can also contain uncertainty about sequence. A feeling might have been present before the message, noticed only afterward, or reconstructed while writing. Keeping those possibilities visible prevents the notebook from presenting a memory as a precise instrument trace.

## 4. Action: what you did

Record the action in concrete terms. “Replied ‘Of course’ immediately.” “Opened the conversation again.” “Asked what the sender wanted to discuss.” “Left the message until morning.”

A reply sent is an action; a missed task later that evening is another event. Record later consequences in a separate note below the five fields. Do not compress a whole evening into a single verdict about how well you coped.

It is also possible to record an impulse without mistaking it for an action: “Wanted to ask whether something was wrong; did not ask.” The gap between intention and conduct is part of the description. It does not need an immediate moral explanation.

## 5. Story: what you thought it meant

Give the interpretation a sentence of its own. “The sender was unhappy with me.” “I expected a difficult conversation.” “I thought a delayed answer would look rude.”

A story is not necessarily false. It may turn out to be accurate. The record simply preserves its status at the time: an interpretation, supported to some degree or not yet tested.

Nor does this field claim that interpretation always arrives last. Meaning can be present while an event unfolds. The last column is a place on the page, not a universal timeline of the mind. Keeping it separate lets you ask later what information supported it and what information became available afterward.

## The whole example, held apart

| Field | Illustrative entry |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Received “Can we talk tomorrow?” |
| Signal | Noticed pressure in the chest |
| Felt shift | Settled to preoccupied, as remembered afterward |
| Action | Replied “Of course” immediately |
| Story | Thought the sender was unhappy with me |

The table leaves the sender’s intention unresolved and gives the next inquiry a set of distinctions to work with.

Suppose the conversation turns out to concern an ordinary scheduling question. That new information belongs beside the original record. It need not be used to ridicule the earlier concern. The useful comparison is between what was known, what was inferred and what was later learned.

## Comparing without forcing a pattern

If you want to try the form, record a few low-stakes occurrences when it is convenient. Keep the entries short. You can leave any field blank, stop if the exercise becomes distressing, or decide that a different form of reflection suits you better.

When you compare entries, begin with a small question: does a particular response recur in these records? Count only what is actually written. A similar phrase in two rows is a reason to look more closely, not proof of a stable personal architecture.

Include differences. If one message prompted an immediate reply and another did not, what circumstances were different? Was one sender familiar? Was more context available? Were you busy, rested or already concerned? These are questions for further description; the notebook cannot settle them by itself.

You may find an apparent recurrence. You may find no clear pattern. You may discover that the form leaves out something essential about the situation. Each result can inform whether to keep, change or abandon the method. There is no quota of revelations to extract.

## A map that leaves room for correction

Calling the log a map is an analogy: it selects a few features so that their relations can be examined. A map is useful only for questions its selection can serve. Five columns cannot contain a relationship, establish a motive or replace care and conversation.

What they can offer is a discipline of separation. Here are the words received. Here is what was noticed. Here is the action. Here is the meaning assigned. Here is what remains uncertain.

Before the explanation becomes persuasive, let the record remain particular enough to correct it.
